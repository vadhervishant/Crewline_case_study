How I read the case

2 outages · 1 job · 1 worker

The database is a bystander

Both outages trace to one bulk job on one worker, and that job would overload Postgres too. MongoDB has had multi-document transactions since 4.0. A migration would cost roughly 15–30 eng-months to fix problems we can fix for 4.5.

8 weeks asked · ~21 weeks real

The real deadline is the customer's rollout

The contract is unsigned and their rollout starts in about five months (around week 21). That gives us room to re-scope now. "Segregated database" is most likely a security requirement. We can probably meet it without a second stack, and we confirm that before signing.

Platform: 250% if we say yes to all

Three people are the real constraint

Carrier Ingestion (2) and Platform (1) sit under the outages, the export, a dedicated database and SOC 2. The plan works around those three people. Spreading the 22 total evenly would not.

~14 of 36 eng-months to on-call + support

Hiring won't help this quarter. Cutting support load will.

A new hire takes half of an existing engineer for three months, so the first quarter nets out at zero or worse. On-call and support take about 14 eng-months a quarter, so every 10% we remove is worth about 1.4 eng-months, every quarter. We fund that in steps and count it only once measured.

AI: 0 assumed · ≈1.3–2.4 eng-months if measured

Invest in AI. Don't bet the plan on it.

Every engineer gets AI agents under written rules, every merge request gets an AI first review, and support gets an AI assistant in our monitoring tool. The dates assume no speed-up. Time saved is measured at week 6 and already has a destination: support tooling first, then quoting on mobile.

1The database: fix it in place and keep MongoDB

What I'd do

Both outages came from one bulk import job on one oversized server, and that job would overload Postgres too. From week 2, imports run on their own queue and servers, one at a time under a database write cap; by week 10 every carrier runs in small, restartable batches. Reports move to a copy of the database. MongoDB has had multi-step transactions since version 4.0, so all multi-step updates go through one shared helper (the useful part of the shared-layer idea) with automated checks.

Cost

4.5 eng-months, mostly both Ingestion engineers, plus some AWS spend.

Giving up

A relational database for now, new carrier integrations until week 10, and the senior Quoting engineer's preferred plan. They'll own the transaction work and the criteria for revisiting Postgres.

Change my mind

If the database still struggles with imports isolated, we tune and resize MongoDB first. If integrity bugs persist in flows on the helper, or finance needs queries our data model can't serve, we move that one area to Postgres.

2The national broker: yes, re-scoped before we sign

What I'd do

The contract is unsigned and their rollout is about five months out (week 21), so we can reshape it. State-specific quoting rules become a standard feature by week 8; two prospects want them too. The export becomes a configurable feature from week 10, built by Billing so Carrier Ingestion stays on the outages. Instead of a separate database we offer enforced data separation, access logs, encryption and SOC 2. A separate database, if still required, becomes a priced tier, quoted before signing and built before rollout.

Cost

4.0 eng-months, the size of the original ask, spent on product we can sell again.

4Deliverable 4 · Assumptions

The three assumptions the plan leans on most, and how to check each one cheaply.

Ranked by how much of the plan falls over if the assumption is wrong. All three get checked in weeks 1–2, before the plan hardens. AI isn't on this list because no committed date depends on it.

Assumption 1 of 3Decision 1

The outages are an isolation problem, not a database problem.

What rests on it: the whole case against Postgres. If it's wrong, we spend 4.5 eng-months and the outages continue.

About 3 daysIngestion + PlatformWeeks 1–2

Cheap check

  1. Week 1, half a day: pull database and queue metrics from both outage nights (CPU, disk I/O, cache pressure, lock waits, connections, Redis queue depth).
  2. Week 2, two to three days: replay an anonymized copy of the worst night's carrier file on a production-sized staging environment, once as it runs today and once isolated under the write cap. Measure broker-app p95 latency both times.
  3. Confirm our MongoDB version and setup support transactions (4.0 or later on a replica set, 4.2 or later if sharded).

It holds if

The isolated replay keeps broker-app latency within about 10% of normal.

If it doesn't

In week 3 the senior Quoting engineer and Platform tune and resize MongoDB. If the data model turns out to be the cause, we scope moving that one area.

The national broker cares about its rollout date and data security, not a separate database by week 8.

What rests on it: the re-scope, and with it $480k a year. They'd likely be our largest customer, about twice the average top-10 account.

How the fix works

  1. Stopgap by W2. The existing job moves to its own worker and queue, with a cap on write batch size and concurrency, an off-peak schedule, and alerts on API p95, replication lag and queue depth. It is cheap and reversible.
  2. Keep the raw file. Each carrier file lands unchanged in versioned, encrypted S3 with a checksum and a manifest record.
  3. Check the format before touching live data. Columns, types, row count and trailer totals are compared with that carrier's last known format. A failing file is quarantined and alerts, so a silent format change can't corrupt live policies.
  4. Parse outside the database. Workers normalize records from S3, so parsing and validation put no load on MongoDB.
  5. Keep each policy in order. Records are grouped by policy and applied in file order by one worker, so an endorsement and a cancellation land in the right order. Different policies run in parallel.
  6. Make every write idempotent. Each record gets a fixed id (carrier, file, line) and applies only if it is newer than what the policy already holds. Checkpoints mean a failed chunk resumes and can't double-apply.
  7. Share one write budget. Workers scale with the queue but share one rate limit (a Redis token bucket) that backs off when replication lag or write latency rises. More workers never means more load on the database.
  8. Give imports their own Redis. It is set to refuse new jobs when full instead of evicting old ones, and jobs carry S3 references, not rows. Binding and email jobs can't be starved or silently dropped.
  9. Check every file afterwards. Rows applied are compared with rows expected, and we track data freshness per carrier.
  10. Keep reports off the primary. A priority-0 replica tagged for analytics can never become primary. Reports use a secondary-only read preference with that tag, so they fail rather than fall back to the primary, and every report shows its "data as of" time.
  11. Make transactions hard to misuse. A withTransaction() helper wraps the driver's session API, which already retries transient errors. It keeps the session in AsyncLocalStorage (Mongoose 8 has a built-in option for this), so every write inside joins the transaction without passing it by hand. The callback has no side effects, which keeps the automatic retry safe. Transactions stay short, the bind path retries on write conflicts, and tests fail any write made outside the session. Needs MongoDB 4.0+ on a replica set, or 4.2+ if sharded.
  12. Send side effects through an outbox. Carrier files, emails and jobs are written to an outbox in the same transaction and sent once after commit, with an idempotency key. No database can roll back a file that has already been sent.

Tripwires for revisiting Postgres

  • With imports isolated and MongoDB tuned and resized, broker-app p95 latency during imports is still more than 10% above normal, and the cause is how our data is modeled.
  • Any data-integrity incident in a flow already on the transaction helper.
  • Commission or finance reporting needs relational queries that a replica or a small warehouse can't serve.
  • Our MongoDB version or hosting can't run transactions, and upgrading would cost more than moving one area.

Even if one trips, the move is one domain at a time, starting with commissions as a ledger. The whole monolith never moves at once. The senior Quoting engineer owns this list.

The merge-request path: faster, with people still deciding

What the webhooks do

  • Opened or updated. Path rules label the risk zone and request reviewers from the code-owners file: one person, or two on red paths. Branch protection enforces this, not the AI. The AI first review posts within minutes while CI runs.
  • Waiting. With no first review after one working day, the reviewer gets a Slack nudge. After two days, it comes to me.
  • Released. The release watch compares the scorecard for 30 minutes before and after. A breach alerts on-call with the merge request linked.
  • Rule or skill file changed. Two approvals, one from Quoting D as SOC 2 owner.

Targets against the W1 baseline: median time to first review under 4 working hours, time from open to merge down 30%, and no rise in review rounds or failed changes.

What the AI first review checks

  • Every data access carries the customer's tenant id.
  • Multi-step writes run inside the transaction helper.
  • Imports, payouts and outbound files carry an idempotency key.
  • No customer data or secrets in code, logs or prompts.
  • No new dependency without approval.
  • Tests cover the change.

Each finding is fixed or dismissed with a reason. Code goes to the approved vendor under our data agreement. Customer data never does.

Support and on-call: AI in the monitoring tool

What it does

  • When an alert fires or a ticket is escalated, it gathers that customer's logs, metrics, recent imports, releases and merge requests, plus similar past cases. It then posts a first diagnosis with links and the matching runbook step.
  • Support engineers can ask in plain English, such as "failed imports for this broker last night".
  • After an incident, it drafts the timeline for the review.
  • Target: the median time from escalation to first diagnosis halves against the W1 baseline, and escalations to engineers fall 25% with the self-serve tools.
  • When: our monitoring tool's built-in AI goes on in W3, which I set up and Quoting D checks. Quoting C builds the assistant in W6–W8, inside support tooling.

Limits and checks

  • It only reads, one customer at a time, through the same tenant layer we harden for the national broker. It can't change data, restart jobs or message customers. A person acts.
  • Customer data stays with the monitoring vendor only if our contract covers its AI features with no training on our data. Otherwise it runs on models in our AWS account. Every query is logged.
  • Tickets and carrier files can contain malicious text. Because the assistant only reads, the worst case is a bad suggestion that a person ignores.
  • Before launch it is tested on 50 past escalations and both outage nights, and it must point to the right cause in at least 8 of 10.

Measuring AI, and when we stop

How we measure it

  1. W1 baseline from the last two quarters: time against estimate on comparable work, time to first review, review rounds, failed changes, bugs after release, escalations and time to first diagnosis.
  2. From W2, every team uses agents on the work they're allowed to touch. We measure teams, never individuals.
  3. W6: we measure time saved against estimates made before the work started, because cycle time alone can fall just because changes get smaller. That time is released down each team's list for W7–W9, and W10 re-measures for W10–W12.
  4. Stop rule: if a team's failed changes, bugs after release or review time stay above baseline for two weeks, its agent work pauses until fixed. Team leads, Broker App first, can switch off any workflow that slows them.
  5. If it misses, we've spent 0.5 eng-months and about $7–14k by the W6 review. The tests, guides and replay check still ship.

Where data may go

  • Approved vendor, under a data processing agreement with no retention: source code, merge requests, and synthetic or redacted test files.
  • Only models in our AWS account, every call logged: anything with customer or policyholder data, such as carrier files, failed import records, logs and support tickets.
  • The monitoring vendor's AI only if our contract covers it with no training on our data.
  • Agents never write to production. They propose and a person approves.
  • At most two agent-written merge requests in review per reviewer, so review doesn't become the new bottleneck.
  • AI tools are reviewed as SOC 2 vendors, and their audit logs count as evidence. The rules are locked by W7, so the auditor sees one change process for the whole window.
  • AI is never the only control on money movement, policy binding or compliance evidence.

Questions I expect about AI

If AI makes us faster, why not plan on it?Results range from large gains to 19% slower on codebases like ours. Planning on it would put the board statement on a guess. Measuring first, with the time already assigned, gets the upside without that risk.
Doesn't AI make Postgres cheap now?It speeds up rewriting queries. It doesn't speed up the data migration, running both databases side by side, or the cutover, which still sit on the same two Ingestion engineers. The tripwires in Appendix A stay as they are.
Can AI replace the hires?Maybe one. The integrations role is decided at W6 on our own numbers. Not the platform role: AI can't be the second person on a production change or the backup on call.
Why can't AI deliver the full mobile app?It needs 9.0 eng-months from a team with 5.5. Even the strong case adds about 0.7 there, and store review doesn't speed up.
Won't people game the numbers?We measure teams, not individuals, against estimates made before the work started, not counts of merge requests.
